# Professionalism at Focus Bear Reflection

## Research & Learn

**Professional vs. unprofessional behaviour in a casual work environment**
A relaxed culture doesn't mean low standards; it just means the standards show up differently. Professional behaviour looks like showing up prepared, communicating clearly and on time, owning mistakes, and treating "casual" as a licence to be human, not a licence to be careless. Unprofessional behaviour looks like coming to meetings unprepared, going quiet for days without an update, dismissing feedback, or using the relaxed tone as an excuse to be sloppy with deadlines or disrespectful in how you talk to people.

**Respectful communication in remote teams**
In remote/async teams, tone carries almost all the weight since there's no body language to soften a message. That means writing messages that are clear and specific rather than terse or ambiguous, since ambiguity reads as curtness over text. It means assuming good intent when reading a blunt message from a teammate, since text strips out warmth that would be obvious in person, and raising disagreements as questions ("I'm curious about the reasoning behind X, could you walk me through it?") rather than statements that sound like pushback. It also means matching the urgency of the channel to the urgency of the message, so people aren't left guessing how fast to respond.

**Giving and receiving feedback professionally**
Good feedback focuses on the action or the output, not the person ("this chart's labels are hard to read" rather than "you're bad at charts"). It means assuming good intent on both sides, since the person giving feedback is trying to help the outcome, not attack you, and asking clarifying questions instead of reacting defensively, since "Can you give me an example of where that happened?" gets you further than justifying yourself immediately. Repeated feedback is worth treating as a signal rather than a nuisance: if the same note keeps coming up, something in the process needs to change, not just the next output.

**Working with supervisors guide**
Reviewed the Focus Bear guide on working with supervisors. The key takeaways I want to carry into this internship are coming to every meeting prepared with an agenda and having done the basic homework myself first, and defaulting to self-direction by proposing a next step ("Based on my research, I think I should do X next, is that the right call?") rather than waiting to be told what to do. It also means matching the channel to the urgency, using email for routine things, chat for same-day needs, and SMS or a call only for genuine blockers or emergencies, giving a short end-of-day update by default so there's never a stretch of silence on my end, and taking real notes and following up on action items so I'm not re-asking things I've already been told.

## Reflection

**Great teamwork and professionalism I've experienced**
In my previous role at Wipro on the Nestlé account, the exchanges that worked best were the ones where everyone came in having already done their own digging, so meetings were spent deciding things, not explaining basics. What made it effective was that people flagged blockers early instead of sitting on them, and feedback on dashboards or reports was framed around the deliverable itself rather than who built it.

**Making my communication clear, respectful, and inclusive**
This means writing updates that are specific enough to act on, such as "finished the data cleaning step, starting on the trend analysis next" rather than "made progress," and keeping the tone warm even when being direct, favouring a quick clarifying question over an assumption. It also means not letting the informal culture become an excuse for vague or careless communication.

**Helping create a positive, fun, professional environment**
Being reliable is itself a contribution to a good culture. When people can trust that I'll follow through and give honest updates, it lowers the anxiety and chasing that everyone else has to do. Beyond that, I want to bring genuine curiosity and a willingness to ask good questions, which the guide makes clear is welcomed rather than seen as a burden.

**Preparing effectively for meetings**
Read the relevant brief or onboarding material beforehand, try to answer my own basic questions first through a quick search or checking with ChatGPT, and walk in with a short agenda and a clear sense of where I'm currently blocked or need input. Taking notes during the meeting means I don't need to re-ask the same thing later.

**What being proactive looks like during this internship**
Not waiting to be told the next step by default, and instead proposing a direction and checking it's right. Giving a short update at the end of each working day. Following the guide's escalation ladder when stuck: search it myself first, then ask a teammate, and only go to my supervisor after a genuine 30-minute effort.

**Following up and escalating**
Start with the lowest-friction channel that fits the urgency, using email for non-urgent asks and chat for same-day needs. If there's no response within the expected window (48 hours for email, 24 hours for chat), a polite nudge is welcome rather than awkward. True emergencies, such as something broken, being locked out, or a time-critical issue, go straight to SMS or a call rather than waiting on a slower channel.

**Things I won't do**
I won't sit idle waiting for instructions when I could be exploring or asking, and I won't show up to a meeting without having read the brief or thought through my own progress and blockers first. I won't let feedback go unaddressed if it repeats, since that's a sign I've missed something, not a reason to tune it out. I won't rely on a single communication channel when something is actually urgent, and I won't ask questions I could answer myself with a 10-second search.
